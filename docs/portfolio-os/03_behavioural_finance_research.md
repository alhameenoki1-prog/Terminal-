# Document 3 — Scientific Behavioural Finance Research Report

## 1. Executive summary

**Conclusions first.** Transaction and brokerage data can establish *behavioural regularities* for one person with real confidence — a spending response to income arrival, a payday cycle, under-diversification, a tendency to realise gains before losses, inattention to recurring charges — but they cannot establish *psychological causes*. The academic literature that Portfolio OS would draw on is itself in flux: prospect theory's choice patterns replicate across 19 countries (E1), yet the magnitude and even the universality of loss aversion are contested (E1 meta-analysis gives a coefficient of about 1.96, while serious critics argue it is context-dependent); present bias in money choices is small once measured carefully; and the pooled "nudges work" effect size (d ≈ 0.45) collapses toward zero after publication-bias correction, while large government trials still find real but modest effects (1.4 percentage points on average). A personal tool must therefore be built on the *observable* layer, speak in the language of patterns and ranges, test every alternative explanation that the founder's Nigeria–UAE context makes plausible (inflation hedging, family obligations, informal savings groups, transfer levies, misclassified transfers), and treat every intervention as an experiment on one person rather than a settled remedy.

**What we recommend.** (1) Build a "behavioural claims ladder" with five rungs — observed, measured pattern, evidence-supported explanation, hypothesis, not establishable — and hard-code which sentences the product may generate at each rung. (2) Ship, in the personal MVP, only measures with transparent arithmetic: income-arrival spending response, payday-cycle index, recurring-charge audit, cash-buffer in months, portfolio concentration/currency exposure, and realised-gain/loss asymmetry (when brokerage records exist). (3) Pair the behavioural ledger with two short validated questionnaires (the SOEP general risk question and the CFPB 10-item Financial Well-Being Scale) because stated preferences carry information that behaviour alone does not. (4) Run every intervention — payday plan prompts, friction on discretionary transfers, goal earmarking — as a pre-registered within-person alternating design with explicit stopping rules and no shaming. (5) Never emit personality labels, "bias scores", or single-number "financial health" grades; the evidence does not license them for an individual.

**Biggest uncertainty.** Statistical power for one person. Our synthetic study shows six pay cycles detect only large payday effects (≥30% uplift) and that a 15% intervention effect needs more than a year of alternating months to be detected reliably. The honest product consequence is slow, provisional language and long evaluation horizons — which conflicts with the product team's likely desire for early "insights". We preserve that disagreement in §7.

## 3. Findings

Each finding states the evidence grade, the source numbers (see §10), and — where it bears on the product — which rung of the claims ladder (§4.1) it supports.

### 3A. Theoretical foundations, evidence strength and current critiques

**F1. Prospect theory's choice patterns replicate; its parameters are not portable to an individual.** Kahneman & Tversky (1979) and the cumulative version (Tversky & Kahneman 1992) describe reference dependence, diminishing sensitivity, loss aversion and probability weighting [S1, S2]. A pre-registered 19-country replication (N = 4,099) reproduced 94% of the 17 original choice items and 12 of 13 theoretical contrasts, with attenuated magnitudes [S3] — **E1** for the qualitative patterns. Barberis's review documents that applying the theory outside the laboratory requires auxiliary assumptions about what the reference point is and how outcomes are bracketed, which is where applications fail [S4] (**E3**). *Product implication:* the system may use reference dependence to *frame hypotheses* (for example, "purchase price appears to act as a reference point in your selling decisions") but may not estimate a personal value-function curvature from transactions; there is no identified estimator for that from spending data.

**F2. Loss aversion exists on average but its magnitude and generality are contested.** A 2024 meta-analysis of 607 estimates from 150 articles places the mean loss-aversion coefficient at 1.955 (95% interval 1.820–2.102) [S8] (**E1**). Against this, Gal & Rucker (2018) argue that the evidence does not support losses being generally more impactful than gains [S5]; Walasek & Stewart (2015) show loss aversion can be made to disappear or reverse by changing the distribution of gains and losses people sample from, supporting a decision-by-sampling account [S6]; Mrkva et al. (2020; N = 17,720 across five samples) find loss aversion present at all knowledge levels but moderated by domain knowledge, experience and age [S7]. The endowment effect and status quo bias are the classic field manifestations [S9, S34]. *Grade for the construct:* **E1 that an asymmetry exists on average; E2–E4 on its size for any given person and context.* *Product implication:* never assign an individual a loss-aversion coefficient; the construct's moderators (experience, stake size, context) are precisely the things that vary within one person's financial life.

**F3. Mental accounting and mental budgeting have strong field support from transaction data — and known side effects.** Thaler's framework [S10, S11] and the behavioural life-cycle hypothesis [S12] predict non-fungibility across labelled accounts. Field tests using panel transaction data reject fungibility: households substitute to lower-grade petrol when prices rise beyond what income effects explain, best fitted by category budgeting [S14]; the marginal propensity to consume food out of US SNAP benefits is 0.5–0.6 versus much lower out of cash, even for households for which the benefit is economically equivalent to cash [S15]; small windfalls (a grocery coupon) raise spending on items not previously purchased [S16]; consumers set category budgets and under-consume typical items late in the budget period [S13] (**E1** for non-fungibility; multiple independent designs). Side effect: earmarked savings can be *over-protected* — people borrow at high interest rather than draw on savings labelled for a goal, believing it the responsible choice [S17] (**E2**, six lab studies). Recent reviews of budgeting behaviour confirm that budgeting is common, imprecise and category-based [S161] (**E3**). *Product implication:* labelled sub-accounts and "envelopes" are evidence-backed tools, but the product must warn when a labelled buffer is being protected at the cost of expensive debt.

**F4. Present bias is real in effort and consumption, small in money-earlier-or-later choices, and predictive of credit behaviour.** Quasi-hyperbolic discounting [S18, S19] motivates commitment products. Measurement has matured: the convex-time-budget meta-analysis of 220 estimates from 28 studies finds people present-biased on average with large heterogeneity, modest selective reporting toward *over-reporting* bias, and reward type (monetary versus non-monetary) as the main moderator [S21]; money-dated choices show "very limited time inconsistency" while real-effort choices show considerably more, and effort-based present bias predicts demand for commitment [S22]; the JEL review by Cohen, Ericson, Laibson & White explains why money-earlier-or-later choices measure required rates of return on financial flows rather than pure time preference [S20] (**E1** for the phenomenon; **E2** for any specific parameter). Field correlates: incentivised present-bias measures predict having and holding more credit-card debt, controlling for income and credit constraints [S23]; account-level data show present-biased planning in credit-card pay-down [S24] (**E2** each). *Product implication:* the product may test for behavioural signatures *consistent with* present bias (spending that front-loads after income, repeated deferral of planned contributions) but must not infer a discount parameter; and it must check the rational alternatives in F15 first.

**F5. Overconfidence and excess trading: strong brokerage evidence, mechanism partly inferred.** Among 66,465 US discount-brokerage households (1991–1996), the most active quintile earned 11.4% a year against a market return of 17.9%; the average household turned over 75% of its portfolio annually [S25]; men traded 45% more than women and trading reduced men's net returns by 2.65 percentage points a year versus 1.72 for women [S26]; Odean (1999) showed stocks bought underperformed stocks sold even ignoring costs [S27] (**E1** for the turnover–performance relation; **E2** for overconfidence as *the* explanation, since gender is a proxy). *Product implication:* turnover, and the realised return of positions bought versus sold, are directly observable and may be reported as facts with costs attached; "overconfidence" is a hypothesis label, not a finding.

**F6. The disposition effect is robust; its cause is not.** Shefrin & Statman named it [S29]; Odean (1998) measured it in 10,000 accounts as a higher proportion of gains realised (PGR) than losses realised (PLR), not explained by rebalancing, trading costs or subsequent performance, and tax-suboptimal except in December [S28] (**E1** for the pattern; replicated in Sweden's full-population wealth data [S33]). Ben-David & Hirshleifer (2012) find the probability of selling is V-shaped in profit — at short holding periods investors are *more* likely to sell big losers than small ones — with no jump at zero, which "provides no clear indication that realization preference explains trading" and points to belief revision instead [S30]. Hartzmark (2015) shows people sell their extreme best and extreme worst positions ("rank effect"), driven by salience of portfolio rank rather than the return level [S31]; Frydman & Rangel (2014) reduce the effect experimentally by making purchase price less salient [S32] (**E2** each). *Product implication:* report PGR−PLR with holding-period conditioning and the V-shape check; attribute to "a pattern consistent with several documented tendencies (realisation preference, belief updating, rank salience)" rather than to "loss aversion".

**F7. Framing, anchoring, salience and attention: well-established in choice experiments, measurable in investing, hard to measure in spending.** Framing and anchoring come from the heuristics-and-biases programme [S36, S37, S164]; salience theory formalises overweighting of salient payoffs [S40]. Attention-driven buying is directly measurable: individual investors are net buyers of attention-grabbing stocks (news, abnormal volume, extreme returns) [S38], and intense buying by Robinhood users forecasts negative returns (average 20-day abnormal return −4.7% for the top stocks bought each day) [S39] (**E1** for attention effects in retail buying). Selective attention to finances ("ostrich effect") is measurable from login data: logins fall 9.5% after market declines [S54, S55], and in bank data attention decreases discretely as balances go negative [S56] (**E2**, two independent datasets). *Product implication:* app-open and account-check timestamps are legitimate behavioural data if the user consents; buying concentrated in high-attention securities is reportable.

**F8. Pain of paying and payment mechanism.** Prospective accounting of debt versus consumption [S41], higher willingness to pay with cards than cash [S42] and reduced spending salience under less transparent payment modes [S43] are **E2** (lab and field-experiment) findings; the magnitude in cash-light, transfer-heavy Nigerian and UAE payment environments is unknown (**E6**: validate with the founder's own card-versus-transfer split before using).

**F9. Financial literacy: strongly correlated with outcomes; education effects are real but small and decay.** The "Big Three" questions (compound interest, inflation, diversification) show widespread illiteracy worldwide and predict retirement planning [S44, S45] (**E1** correlational). The Fernandes et al. meta-analysis of 201 studies finds financial-education interventions explain 0.1% of variance in downstream behaviour, decaying to negligible effects 20 months out, with stronger associations in correlational studies that shrink when psychological traits are controlled [S46]; the 2022 RCT-only meta-analysis (Kaiser et al.) finds positive effects on knowledge and behaviours — our verification confirmed the paper but we could not retrieve the abstract's numbers in this session, so we cite it for direction only [S47] (**E1** for both). *Product implication:* a literacy check is a worthwhile *covariate* for interpreting behaviour and a trigger for "just-in-time" explanation, not an outcome to train.

**F10. Household finance as a discipline: the normative benchmark matters.** Campbell's address frames household finance as the study of how households *should* and *do* invest; most households are reasonably sensible, a minority make costly mistakes (non-participation, under-diversification, failure to refinance), and mistakes concentrate among the less educated and less wealthy [S48] (**E3**, presidential address synthesising E1/E2 work). Under-diversification is directly measurable in brokerage data and costly to most investors [S52]; naïve 1/n allocation [S51] and home bias [S53] are measurable allocation patterns; financial decision quality peaks in the early fifties [S62]; exponential-growth bias predicts borrowing and saving behaviour [S61]; debt repayment follows a balance-matching heuristic rather than interest-rate ordering [S63] (**E2** each). *Product implication:* the cheapest high-value behavioural outputs are *portfolio-structure facts* (concentration, currency mismatch, cost drag) checked against a stated benchmark, not psychology.

**F11. Myopic loss aversion and evaluation frequency.** More frequent evaluation of returns increases risk aversion in experiments [S49, S50] (**E1** in the lab). For a product this cuts both ways: showing daily portfolio profit-and-loss may raise anxiety and trading; the product should let the user choose the evaluation horizon and should default long-horizon views for long-horizon goals (**E4**, behavioural economist; test as in §4.6).

**F12. Replication context and "scarcity" claims.** Replication rates were 36% for psychology [S57] and 61% for economics laboratory experiments [S58]; replicated effects averaged about half to two-thirds of originals. The "poverty impedes cognitive function" result [S59] sits in a literature where an empirical audit of 20 scarcity-priming studies found "some strong successes and other undeniable failures" [S60] (**E1** for the audit; **E2–E4** for scarcity effects). *Product implication:* claims about *why* the user behaves as they do should carry a lower grade than the original paper's by default; our grading in §4 applies a one-notch discount to any mechanism claim not replicated in field data.

### 3B. What transaction data can and cannot reveal

**F13. Spending responds to the arrival of anticipated income, but much of the "spike" is scheduled spending.** Gelman et al. (2014), using a personal-finance-app panel, find a spending response to anticipated income, but "this apparent excess sensitivity of spending results largely from the coincident timing of regular income and regular spending", with the remainder concentrated among the less liquid [S64]. Olafsson & Pagel (2018) find payday responses that are "robust and homogeneous for all income and spending categories throughout the income distribution", including among people with ample liquidity — the "liquid hand-to-mouth" [S65]. US Social Security recipients spend more in the days after the cheque [S66]; recipients without savings consume 25% fewer calories in the week before the cheque than the week after, while those with savings smooth — about a quarter of the sample [S70]; food-stamp recipients' intake declines over the benefit month [S69]. Alaska dividend recipients show an MPC of about 25% for nondurables within a quarter, *rising* with income and driven by liquid high-income households [S68]. Households spend out of expected tax refunds yet smooth when making expected tax payments — an asymmetry inconsistent with pure liquidity constraints and "most consistent with a mental accounting life-cycle model" [S67]. Spending drops sharply at the predictable exhaustion of unemployment benefits [S71]; consumption elasticity heterogeneity is explained entirely by credit and liquidity in linked-account data [S72]. **E1** (multiple independent transaction datasets, consistent direction). *Five-level reading:* (i) observable — spend by day relative to pay date; (ii) measurable — the ratio and its confidence interval after removing scheduled payments; (iii) supported explanation — a *mix* of liquidity constraints, mental accounting and bounded attention, with the mix differing by person; (iv) hypothesis — which mechanism dominates for this user; (v) not establishable — a discount factor or a "self-control score".

**F14. Attention, fees and recurring charges are among the most tractable targets.** Survey-induced attention to overdraft fees reduces overdrafts for up to two years, by fewer low-balance debit transactions and cancelled recurring withdrawals [S73] (**E2**, quasi-experimental). Card-replacement months, which force active renewal, show much higher subscription cancellation; inertia roughly doubles seller revenue [S74] (**E2**, large payment-network data). Adopting a mobile aggregation app reduced non-sufficient-funds fees — "an unambiguous welfare improvement" [S75]; after installing a mobile app, consumers cut discretionary spending, more so in the evening and among those with lower self-control proxies, consistent with a monitoring channel [S76] (**E2** each; the Levi & Benartzi paper is now in the *Journal of Finance*, 2026). *Product implication:* a recurring-charge audit and a fee ledger are directly observable, high-value and ethically unproblematic.

**F15. Retail investor behaviour from brokerage records: what each metric can carry.** Table 1 applies the five-level test to the standard metrics.

| Metric (from records) | Directly observable | Statistically measurable | Supported explanation | Remains hypothesis | Cannot be inferred |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PGR vs PLR (disposition) [S28, S30] | Realised gains/losses on each sale day against paper gains/losses | PGR−PLR with CI; V-shape by holding period | Realisation preference *or* belief revision *or* rank salience | Which mechanism; tax motive | "Loss aversion coefficient" |
| Turnover, buy-minus-sell return [S25, S27] | Trades, prices, costs | Annual turnover; return of bought vs sold positions net of costs | Costly trading | Overconfidence as cause | "Overconfidence score" |
| Concentration / HHI, 1/n, home bias [S51–S53] | Holdings by issuer, sector, currency | Herfindahl index; share domestic; number of names | Under-diversification | Familiarity vs information | "Risk tolerance" from holdings alone |
| Attention-driven buying [S38, S39] | Buys on days of extreme return/news/volume | Share of buys in top-attention decile | Attention effect | Information vs attention | — |
| Rank effect [S31] | Position rank at sale | Sale probability by rank | Salience | — | — |
| Login/ostrich [S54, S56] | Login timestamps vs returns/balances | Login elasticity to negative returns | Selective attention | Anxiety vs rational inattention | "Avoidance personality" |

Conditions: at least 30–50 sale events for a meaningful PGR−PLR [S28 used thousands of accounts; for one account the CI will be wide — see §4.5 note]; prices and costs with provenance; and NGX/DFM/ADX data validated by System C.

**F16. Robo-advice and automation change observed behaviour.** Robo-adviser adoption reduced disposition, trend-chasing and rank effects for previously under-diversified investors [S77] (**E2**). This is evidence that *decision architecture* rather than *insight* moves investor behaviour — relevant to §3F.

### 3C. Alternative explanations and confounders specific to the founder's context

**F17. Inflation and depreciation make fast spending and dollar conversion rational.** Nigeria's headline inflation was 14.63% year-on-year in September 2026 (NBS, released 15 October 2026; food 19.59%; month-on-month 0.06%), down from 20.98% a year earlier; the NBS dashboard shows core inflation of 12.95% while press coverage printed 13.29% — a discrepancy to be resolved against the NBS PDF [S103] (**E2**, official statistic via press and NBS site). The official NFEM rate was about ₦1,346.91 per US dollar on 29 October 2026 (parallel market about ₦1,385) [S104] (**E3**, press tracker; approximate). Under such conditions, converting naira income to US dollars or AED soon after receipt, buying durables early, or pre-paying annual obligations are *minimum-variance portfolio* choices, not present bias: financial dollarisation is the rational response to inflation-volatile domestic currency [S101] (**E2**). *Rule:* any payday-window outflow to FX, inflation-linked assets, or pre-payment is classified as *hedging/committed*, not discretionary, before any behavioural statistic is computed.

**F18. Family, kin and community obligations are economically large and behaviourally rational.** Pressure to share income is measured as an effective "kinship tax": in Kenyan villages women conceal income at a cost, implying an anticipated tax above 4%, nearly doubling when kin observe income directly [S89]; a third of 361 Kenyan entrepreneurs face distortionary pressure to share, with aggregate productivity losses of about a quarter [S90]; Senegalese experiments show income hiding to avoid informal redistribution [S91]; South African households with more dependants hold non-sharable durables and fewer liquid savings [S92]; Northern Nigerian credit markets function as state-contingent insurance between households [S87, S88] (**E1** across settings for the phenomenon; Nigerian evidence is E2 and from the 1980s–90s). Remittances respond to control and labelling: migrants given more control over home-country accounts saved more without cutting remittances [S96]; matched educational remittances crowded in spending [S97]; reviews [S98]. *Rule:* transfers to named individuals, recurring outbound transfers around payday, and contributions to ajo/esusu/adashe groups must be tagged "obligation/social" and analysed as a separate account with its own baseline — never as "leakage".

**F19. Informal savings groups (ROSCAs) are commitment devices, not anomalies.** Rotating savings and credit associations protect savings from intra-household and kin claims [S85] and are widespread in West Africa [S86]; EFInA's A2F 2023 reports 64% formal inclusion, 10% informal-only use, and 26% exclusion of Nigerian adults, with a 2026 headline of 79% inclusion announced on 21 October 2026 [S107] (**E2** for the survey; group-level prevalence figures not retrieved). Kenyan health-savings experiments found that simply providing a safe place to keep money raised health savings by 66%, that earmarking helped mainly for emergencies or for people "frequently taxed by friends and relatives", and that group-based schemes had "very large effects" [S80] (**E2**). *Rule:* an esusu contribution is a *saving* with a commitment feature and an implicit negative-to-zero return; the product should compute its implicit cost against formal alternatives but must not call it irrational.

**F20. Commitment and default evidence from emerging markets is strong — and adoption is selective.** In the Philippines, 28.4% of offered clients took a commitment account and treatment raised balances by 81 percentage points after 12 months [S78]; Kenyan market women used accounts despite withdrawal fees and invested more, while bicycle-taxi men did not [S79]; mobile money (M-PESA) protected consumption against shocks for users (non-users' consumption fell 7%) [S82], lifted about 194,000 Kenyan households (2%) out of poverty [S81], and protected consumption after rainfall shocks only for users [S83]; mobile-money savings for Mozambican farmers [S84]; default enrolment in Afghanistan raised participation by 40 percentage points — "equivalent to a 50% match" — driven by present bias and cognitive cost [S94]; being paid into an account rather than cash raised savings 131% in India [S95]; commitment accounts for Malawian farmers raised input use [S100]. But merely opening basic accounts in Uganda, Malawi and Chile produced 17%, 10% and 3% active users and no detectable average effects [S93]. Reviews: [S99] (**E1** for commitment and defaults in low- and middle-income settings; **E2** per country). *Implication:* defaults and commitment are the interventions with the best cross-cultural evidence; "access to information" alone is not.

**F21. Transfer levies, fees and data gaps mechanically distort behavioural metrics.** From 1 January 2026 a ₦50 stamp duty applies to electronic transfers of ₦10,000 and above, now borne by the *sender* (previously the recipient under the Electronic Money Transfer Levy), with salary and intra-bank self-transfers exempt [S105] (**E3**, press reporting of bank notices; the Tax department must confirm against the Nigeria Tax Act 2025); a reported April 2026 CBN guide setting transfers above ₦10,000 at ₦60 could not be fetched [S106] (**unverified**). Consequences for measurement: (a) transaction *counts* understate activity if users batch transfers to avoid per-transfer levies; (b) levy lines must be excluded from "spending"; (c) round-number clustering just below ₦10,000 is a fee response, not a budgeting heuristic. Data gaps — cash withdrawals, informal income, point-of-sale agent transactions, inter-account transfers that appear as spending — are the single largest threat to validity; System A must deliver a reconciled transfer-matching layer before any behavioural statistic is shown (**E5**: test = share of outflows that are matched transfers; target >95% of inter-account flows matched).

**F22. Preferences differ across countries and within them, and are not fully stable.** The Global Preference Survey (80,000 people, 76 countries) finds "even larger within-country heterogeneity" than between-country differences in risk and time preference [S102]; reviews of test–retest stability show experimental risk and time measures are only moderately stable, with survey measures often more stable [S118] (**E1**). *Implication:* no national prior ("Nigerians are X") is admissible for an individual; any prior used in Bayesian updating (§4.4) must be weak and disclosed.

| Financial fragility item [S123] | 1 item: confidence in raising $2,000 in 30 days | — | Predicts hardship | Currency/context adaptation needed | Adopt (NGN/AED/USD versions) |

**F24. When a questionnaire adds information beyond behaviour.** Stated preferences and behaviour correlate weakly [S115]; this is not a flaw to be engineered away but a reason to measure both: (a) stated risk willingness tells the product what *benchmark* to judge portfolio structure against; (b) financial well-being is a subjective outcome that balances cannot reveal (a user can be liquid and anxious, or illiquid and content); (c) a planning-propensity measure distinguishes "forgot" from "chose"; (d) literacy tells the explanation layer how much to explain. Questionnaires should be short, repeated at long intervals (annual for traits, quarterly for well-being), and never used to generate labels (§4.7). **E4** (psychometrician and consumer financial-psychology specialist, jointly).

### 3E. Single-user science

**F25. Group results do not transfer to the individual unless the process is ergodic.** Molenaar's idiographic manifesto [S125] and Fisher et al. (2018) — within-person variance two to four times larger than between-person variance across six repeated-measures datasets — mean that population effect sizes are a *prior*, not a prediction, for one person [S126] (**E1**). n-of-1 methods exist to address this: observational and interventional single-case designs (AB, ABAB, alternating treatments, multiple baseline) are catalogued for health behaviour [S127]; n-of-1 randomised crossover trials are an established clinical methodology [S128, S129]; reporting standards (CENT 2015) [S130] and What Works Clearinghouse design standards (minimum three demonstrations of effect, minimum data points per phase) [S131] exist (**E3**). Interrupted time series with segmented regression is the standard quasi-experimental design for a single series with a known intervention date, with explicit handling of autocorrelation, seasonality and an *a priori* impact model [S132, S133] (**E3**). Statistical process control — Shewhart individuals charts and run charts with shift and crossings rules — distinguishes common-cause variation from special-cause change with a controlled false-alarm rate, and is validated by simulation [S134, S135] (**E2/E3**). Multiple-comparison control [S136], the garden of forking paths [S137], low power inflating false discoveries [S138] and Bayesian estimation with explicit priors and regions of practical equivalence [S139] are the relevant statistical safeguards (**E3**). *Defensible confidence language for one person* follows in §4.4.

### 3F. Interventions

**F26. Defaults and automatic escalation: the strongest field evidence.** Automatic enrolment raised 401(k) participation dramatically and anchored contribution rates at the default [S35]; Save More Tomorrow raised savings rates among joiners substantially over successive pay rises [S140] (the abstract was not retrievable; the magnitudes commonly quoted — 3.5% to 13.6% over 40 months — are **recalled, not verified in this session** and should be checked before quotation); emerging-market default results in F20 [S94, S95]. **E1** for defaults. Peer-information nudges can *backfire*: telling low savers how many peers participate reduced enrolment among the ineligible-for-auto-enrolment group, by discouragement from upward comparison [S141] (**E2**). Reminders raise commitment-savings attainment, especially when they mention the goal and incentive; gain/loss framing made no difference [S151] (**E1**, three banks). Reminders for credit-card due dates cut late fees 14% but raised overdraft fees 9%, with net *higher* fees for prior overdraft users [S152] (**E2**) — side effects are real.

**F27. The nudge effect-size debate.** Mertens et al. report d = 0.45 (95% CI 0.39–0.52) over 450+ effect sizes and n ≈ 2.1 million, with "decision structure" interventions (defaults, ordering) outperforming information and assistance nudges, and moderate publication bias [S142]. Maier et al. apply robust Bayesian meta-analysis and conclude there is "no evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias" [S143]; Szaszi et al. argue heterogeneity makes a single mean meaningless and the bias-adjusted mean is near zero [S144]; the earlier Hummel & Maedche review found a majority of studies significant with widely varying sizes [S146]. DellaVigna & Linos compare 126 Nudge Unit RCTs (23 million people) with academic trials: 1.4 percentage points (8.0% over control) versus 8.7 points (33.4%), with selective publication plus low power explaining about 70% of the gap [S145] (**E1**, the most credible point estimate for "a typical nudge at scale"). Opower-style feedback shows action-and-backsliding cycles that attenuate, with effects persisting at 10–20% annual decay after discontinuation [S154] (**E1**). *Working prior for Portfolio OS:* a well-designed informational or reminder nudge should be expected to move a behaviour by low single-digit percentage points; defaults and friction by much more; and effects to decay without reinforcement.

**F28. Goals, implementation intentions and earmarking.** Setting savings goals in a fintech app increased savings rates without reducing savings outside the app, most for the lowest-propensity savers, via monitoring and concreteness [S150] (**E2**, randomised beta-test design). Implementation intentions ("if–then" plans) have a medium-to-large meta-analytic effect on goal attainment across 94 tests [S148] (**E1**; exact d recalled as ≈0.65, not verified here). Goal-setting theory supports specific, difficult goals with feedback [S149] (**E1**, 35-year programme). Earmarking into partitioned envelopes with a visual reminder raised savings among low-income Indian workers [S153] (**E2**). Commitment devices are reviewed by Bryan, Karlan & Nelson [S147]; the classic self-imposed-deadline study is **retracted** [S160] and must not be cited as support. Age-progressed future-self renderings increased later-reward choices in four studies [S124] (**E2**, lab).

**F29. Boosting and ethics.** Hertwig & Grüne-Yanoff distinguish nudges (steering) from boosts (building competence, e.g., teaching a rule of thumb) [S155]; Sunstein catalogues why nudges fail (strong preferences, counter-nudges, reactance) [S156]; disclosing a default nudge did not eliminate its effect, which bears on transparency [S157]; Loewenstein & Chater and Bhargava & Loewenstein argue that nudges are a complement to, not a substitute for, structural remedies [S158, S159] (**E3**). *Design rule:* every behavioural feature must be transparent, reversible, opt-in, and offered with its evidence grade; the system informs and offers architecture, it does not persuade.

### 4.3 Minimum data and power rules (statistician's specification)

- **Independence unit is the pay cycle, not the day.** Daily spending is autocorrelated and clustered around events; tests must resample or permute at cycle level or use circular shifts of the event indicator, and regression standard errors must be heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation consistent.
- **Pre-specify the impact model** (level shift, slope change, both) before any intervention, as ITS practice requires [S132]; store it in the ledger as a "pre-registration record" with timestamp.
- **Run-chart rules for monitoring**: with 10–12 weekly points, the shift rule (≥ 7–8 consecutive points on one side of the median, depending on n) and crossings rule hold a false-signal rate near 5%; the trend rule is "virtually useless" [S135]. Use individuals charts (mean ± 3σ from the moving range) for weekly totals [S134].
- **False-discovery control across the catalogue**: with 15 constructs computed monthly, control the false discovery rate at 10% with Benjamini–Hochberg [S136] per reporting cycle, and report the number of tests run.
- **Bayesian updating with honest priors**: start each construct at a weak prior centred on the *null* (no effect) with a region of practical equivalence (ROPE) defined in advance (e.g., ±10% for a payday ratio) [S139]; report posterior probability that the effect is outside the ROPE. Population effect sizes from F13–F28 may inform the prior *width*, never its centre (F22, F25).
- **Seasonality**: 12 months of data before any annual-seasonal claim (Ramadan/Eid timing shifts ~11 days a year; December; school fees in January/May/September); inflation-adjust nominal series using the NBS CPI for NGN and the relevant UAE index for AED, with the index value and release date stored.

### 4.5 Worked example: six months, salary on the 25th, "spending spike" analysis (synthetic data)

Set-up (all numbers synthetic; script `payday_sim.py`, seed 20261102): one NGN current account, 1 January–30 June 2026 (181 days). Net salary ₦1,500,000 credited on the 25th or the preceding business day (actual dates 23 Jan, 25 Feb, 25 Mar, 24 Apr, 25 May, 25 Jun). The generator includes: discretionary spending with a weekend uplift of 25%, a *true* payday-window uplift of 45% for days 0–6, an end-of-month squeeze of −15%; annual rent ₦2.4m on 26 January; school fees ₦450k on 28 January and 27 May; utilities on the 2nd; a family transfer of ₦150k within three days of each payday; a US-dollar purchase of ₦300k the day after five of six paydays; weekly ajo of ₦20k on Mondays; an investment contribution of ₦200k two days after payday.

**Step 1 — the naïve statistic is wrong by a factor of five.** Using *all outflows*, mean daily outflow in the payday window is ₦205,417 against ₦25,567 on other days: ratio 8.03 (bootstrap 95% interval 4.3–13.8). This is what an unreconciled app would call a "post-payday splurge". It is almost entirely rent, school fees, the family transfer, the FX purchase and the investment contribution.

**Step 2 — after classification, the discretionary ratio is 1.60 (95% interval 1.38–1.83).** Mean discretionary spend is ₦32,612/day in the window versus ₦20,389/day otherwise.

**Step 3 — is it distinguishable from chance given autocorrelation?** A circular-shift permutation of the window indicator (180 shifts) gives a null 95th percentile of 1.44 and p = 0.006. A log-spend regression with weekday fixed effects and an end-of-month indicator gives a payday coefficient of 0.430 (×1.54), HAC(7) standard error 0.080, t = 5.4. The weekend share is 29.3% in the window and 28.6% outside, so weekday composition does not explain it.

**Step 4 — does it hold cycle by cycle?** Window/rest ratios by cycle: 1.71, 1.86, 1.50, 1.73, 1.45 (sixth cycle incomplete). Five of five complete cycles exceed the ROPE upper bound of 1.10.

**Step 5 — alternative explanations tested and reported.** (a) Scheduled obligations: removed in Step 2 and *explain 93% of the naïve spike*. (b) Family transfer and FX purchase: classified as obligation and hedging, excluded, shown separately (₦150k and ₦300k per cycle). (c) Inflation: at 14.6% annual CPI the within-cycle price drift is ~1.1% a month — immaterial to a 1.6× ratio. (d) Weekday composition: no. (e) Income timing drift: pay dates vary by two days; the window is anchored to the actual credit date. (f) Informal income: none in the synthetic data; in production, unexplained credits in the window would trigger a flag, not an adjustment.

**Step 6 — control-chart view.** Weekly discretionary totals (27 weeks): mean ₦155,241, σ̂ from moving range ₦43,856, upper control limit ₦286,808. No week breaches the limit and the longest run on one side of the median is 3 — i.e., *no special-cause change* over the period; the payday pattern is a stable *within-cycle* rhythm, not a trend. This distinction is exactly what the user should be told.

**Step 7 — what the product may say (L2 wording, generated from templates):**

> "Over five complete pay cycles since January, your discretionary spending in the seven days after salary averaged 1.6 times the rest of the month (likely range 1.4–1.8). This held in every cycle. Bills, school fees, the transfer to [counterparty], your dollar purchase and your investment contribution were excluded — together they account for most of the money that leaves in that week. Bank-data studies find a similar after-income pattern in most people; common contributors are timing of plans, attention and available cash. The system does not know which applies to you. If you would like, it can show a short 'payday plan' prompt the evening before salary for alternating months, so that its effect on this number can be measured over the next year."

**Step 8 — power, stated honestly.** Simulation with the same noise level: with a circular-shift test at α = 0.05, the probability of detecting a true 1.20× uplift is 0.35 with 3 cycles, 0.53 with 6, 0.77 with 12; a 1.30× uplift: 0.60/0.78/0.93; 1.45×: 0.80/0.89/0.97; a 1.10× uplift is essentially undetectable within a year (0.32). For the *intervention*, an alternating-month design detecting a 15% reduction in window spending has power ≈ 0.23 at 6 months and ≈ 0.41 at 12 months; a 30% reduction has 0.68 and 0.93. Product consequence: intervention effects of nudge-typical size (F27) will not be individually detectable in a year; the product must say "no measurable difference yet" rather than "it worked" or "it failed", and should favour interventions with large expected effects (defaults, auto-sweeps, friction) over informational prompts if measurable change is the goal.

**Step 9 — disposition analogue.** For brokerage data the same logic applies: with 30 sale days, a PGR−PLR difference of 0.15 has a bootstrap interval roughly ±0.15 wide (E5 assumption; to be simulated with the Quant team once record formats are known), so the product will usually be at L1 ("more gains than losses realised so far, N sales") rather than L2 for at least a year of active trading.

### 4.6 Intervention protocol (experimental-design specialist)

1. **Eligibility:** only constructs at L2 with a pre-registered impact model; user opts in after seeing the evidence grade and expected effect range (from F26–F28, scaled down per F27).
2. **Design:** alternating treatment by pay cycle (ABAB…), randomised order within pairs, minimum 12 cycles; or multiple-baseline across categories when the intervention cannot be withdrawn (e.g., an auto-sweep).
3. **Outcome:** the catalogue metric plus the CFPB well-being score quarterly; harm metrics pre-specified (overdraft/fees — F26 side effects; protected-buffer borrowing — F3).
4. **Analysis:** permutation test over cycle labels; Bayesian posterior vs ROPE; stopping rule: stop early only for harm.
5. **Reporting to the user:** §4.4 wording; the user can withdraw at any time and the record is kept.
6. **Ethics:** no social comparison (F26 backfire), no loss-framed or shame-framed copy, no streaks or dark patterns, no interventions on obligation/social transfers, full transparency of mechanism (F29).

## 5. Alternatives considered and rejected

| Alternative | Why rejected (for now) |
|---|---|
| **Structural estimation of a personal prospect-theory or β–δ model from transactions** | Not identified from spending data; parameters are context-dependent and unstable (F1, F2, F4, F22). Would produce numbers that look scientific and are not. Could be revisited only with purpose-built incentivised tasks, which the psychometric evidence says are unreliable per person (F23). |
| **Machine-learned "behavioural profile" or clustering of the user against a population** | n = 1 at launch; non-ergodicity (F25); no Nigerian/UAE reference population; label risk (§4.7). Revisit at commercialisation with consented cohorts and pre-registered validation. |
| **Large language model reading transactions and narrating psychology** | Exactly the fabrication path the brief warns about (lesson 4); causes cannot be read off a ledger. The LLM composes only from templated, computed L0–L3 sentences. |
| **Gamified streaks, badges, social leaderboards** | Peer comparison backfires among low savers (F26); dark-pattern and autonomy concerns (F29); no evidence of durable financial benefit at the individual level. |
| **Composite financial-health score** | Hides uncertainty and incommensurability; the validated instrument (CFPB) is a self-report, not a ledger derivative. |
| **Classical between-subject A/B testing** | Impossible at n = 1; replaced by within-person alternating designs and ITS (F25, §4.6). |
| **Daily portfolio profit-and-loss notifications as "engagement"** | Myopic loss aversion evidence (F11) and attention-induced trading (F7) suggest harm; default to goal-horizon views. |
| **Treating ajo/esusu and family transfers as "leakage" to be minimised** | Empirically they are commitment and insurance mechanisms (F18, F19); ethically off-limits. |
| **Importing US item parameters of the CFPB scale as if normed locally** | Accepted only with a visible "US-normed" caveat and as a within-person series; cross-person comparison is rejected until a local validation exists. |

## 7. Risks, open uncertainties and disagreements

**R1. Power versus product expectations (the central disagreement).** *Statistician:* six cycles support only large effects; intervention evaluation needs a year or more; the honest default is silence or "too early". *Behavioural economist:* the user benefits from seeing L0/L1 facts early (recurring charges, fee drag, concentration) which need no inference, and from interventions with large expected effects (defaults, auto-sweeps) whose value does not depend on detecting small changes. *Psychologist:* premature pattern statements shape self-perception and can become self-fulfilling or demoralising; favour the statistician. *Proposed resolution:* ship L0/L1 outputs immediately, hold L2 behind the minimum-sample gates, and let the founder choose whether to see "early, provisional" L1 patterns with explicit labels.

**R2. Is loss aversion a safe foundation?** *Behavioural economist:* the 2024 meta-analysis (λ ≈ 1.96) settles existence on average. *Psychologist:* Gal & Rucker and Walasek & Stewart show the construct is partly an artefact of design and context; building product logic on it risks inheriting a fragile assumption. *Resolution:* use loss aversion only as an L3 hypothesis label; never as a parameter or a design justification on its own.

**R3. Mechanism attribution for the payday pattern.** Gelman et al. attribute most excess sensitivity to scheduled spending; Olafsson & Pagel find responses even among the liquid; Kueng finds high-income, liquid households most responsive; Baugh et al. favour mental accounting. The literature does not agree on the mechanism, so the product must not. *Resolution:* report the decomposition (how much is scheduled) and name all three contributors.

**R4. Nudge effect sizes.** The Mertens/Maier/Szaszi exchange and DellaVigna & Linos together imply expected effects far smaller than popular accounts; this lowers the priority of informational nudges in favour of architecture (defaults, friction). The behavioural economist notes that the founder is a motivated, sophisticated single user for whom self-chosen commitment may work better than average; the statistician replies that this is a hypothesis to test, not a planning assumption.

**R5. Context evidence is thin where it matters most.** African evidence on retail-investor behaviour and on Nigerian household transaction data could not be searched before the search budget ran out; the Nigerian informal-finance evidence we verified is largely from the 1980s–90s or from Kenya/Senegal/South Africa. Any claim that a pattern "is typical" in Nigeria is unsupported.

**R6. Instrument norms.** The CFPB scale and DOSPERT are US-developed; measurement invariance across Nigeria/UAE is unknown. Within-person change scores are defensible; level comparisons are not.

**R7. Data quality is the dominant risk.** Misclassified transfers inflate "spending" by multiples (Step 1 of §4.5 shows 8× versus 1.6×). Behavioural outputs must be disabled until System A's matching quality metric is met.

**R8. Ethics and autonomy.** Even transparent nudges influence behaviour (F29); the line between "architecture the user asked for" and "manipulation" must be drawn by Legal/Ethics with the user, and revisited before any commercialisation.

**R9. Retracted and unverified sources.** One canonical commitment-device study is retracted (S160); two magnitudes in F26 and F28 were recalled rather than verified in this session and are marked; Besley, Coate & Loury (1993) could not be verified (S162). These do not change conclusions but must be cleaned up before external use.

**R10. Unknowns the founder must confirm:** tax residency, income regularity, whether brokerage records with cost basis are available for NGX/DFM/ADX holdings, and consent to app telemetry.

## 9. Required validation before implementation

1. **Transfer-matching quality gate** (System A): ≥95% of inter-account flows matched on three months of the founder's real data; behavioural module disabled below the gate.
2. **Classification audit**: founder labels 200 sampled transactions; category and obligation/hedge tags must reach ≥90% agreement before L1 statistics are shown.
3. **Re-run the §4.5 pipeline on real data** with the pre-registered ROPE (±10%) and report power actually achieved given the observed noise (σ of log daily spend).
4. **Precision simulation for brokerage metrics** with the Quant team using the real trade-count distribution.
5. **Questionnaire pilot**: founder completes SOEP, Big Three, fragility item and CFPB-10; retest after four weeks to establish personal measurement error before any change is interpreted.
6. **Template red-team**: generate 100 synthetic ledgers (including adversarial cases: missing months, salary date drift, large one-off transfers, high inflation months) and confirm no sentence exceeds its rung.
7. **Legal/ethics review** of the intervention protocol and consent text; Tax confirmation of F21 facts and dates.
8. **Source clean-up**: verify the two recalled magnitudes (F26, F28), resolve the NBS core-inflation discrepancy (F17), fetch the CBN April 2026 fee circular, and replace or drop S162.
9. **Freshness tests**: every quoted statistic (CPI, FX, levy) stored with source, release date and a scheduled re-check; stale-constant failure mode from the context brief must be impossible by construction.
